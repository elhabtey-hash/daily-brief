Ukraine
Mixed

Kremlin will not confirm Trump's energy ceasefire. Zelensky says Russia already broke it. EU adds almost 1,600 sanctions listings

What happened

Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said on Monday 12 October that 'contacts are ongoing' and Moscow would say more 'as soon as we have anything to say', declining to confirm Trump's Sunday claim that both sides had agreed an immediate energy ceasefire (TASS, Interfax via Ukrainska Pravda, Kyiv Independent). He rejected Zelensky's proposal for a 60-day ceasefire to hold elections, saying Russia wants 'long-term peace' not a ceasefire, though Putin could order no strikes on an election day. Zelensky told the Warsaw Security Forum that Russia had already violated the truce, with strikes on Kharkiv's CHP-5 heat and power plant and a Kyiv power plant, and said 'if Russia does not agree to an energy ceasefire, we will respond' (Al Jazeera, AP). He said Ukraine had held off strikes on Russian energy since Sunday, and that because Ukraine has only one refinery, any truce must stop Russian attacks on its power grid. Russia's deputy prime minister Alexander Novak said Moscow and Washington are discussing diesel supplies and Russian diesel exports could reach 3 to 3.5 million tonnes by year-end (Newsquawk). In Brussels the EU agreed its largest-ever package of listings: 1,569 entries, 826 companies and 743 executives in Russia's military-industrial complex, focused on ballistic missile production, plus 77 people over the Duma 'elections' in occupied Ukrainian territory (Council of the EU press releases, 12 Oct).

Context (what it sits on)

Trump's announcement came days after his deal to buy Russian diesel under a US sanctions waiver, which Kyiv and EU capitals criticised. The energy war matters to winter: Russia targets Ukraine's grid, Ukraine targets Russian refineries.

Forward (months–years)

Watch for a Kremlin statement, whether strikes on the grid stop, whether Ukraine resumes refinery strikes, and the European Council on Thursday 15 and Friday 16 October.

Mixed

IRGC says third supertanker set ablaze in Hormuz in two days. Six ships reported hit on Sunday. Threat rated severe

What happened

Iran's Revolutionary Guard navy said a supertanker that switched off its transponder and ignored warnings was hit in the Strait of Hormuz on Monday morning and caught fire, the third 'offending' tanker in two days, and warned that ships cooperating with the US military would face a 'decisive response' (Sepah News via Anadolu, Al Arabiya/AFP). It named no ship. The UK Maritime Trade Operations centre (UKMTO) issued five more attack warnings for Sunday 11 October, so that six commercial vessels, five tankers and a container ship, were reported struck that day, one near Sohar in Oman by a drone (UKMTO warnings 164 to 169-26, gCaptain). Another outbound products tanker was reported hit on Monday (UKMTO 168-26). The joint maritime information centre keeps the threat at SEVERE, its second-highest level.

Context (what it sits on)

Iran has restricted Hormuz since the US-Israeli strikes began in February, and the US runs a counter-blockade of Iranian ports. Tehran called the latest US reply on its seven conditions for reopening insufficient on Sunday. Trump has pledged no US strikes before the 3 November midterms.

Forward (months–years)

Watch the number of ships hit, insurance rates, any US response, and whether talks via Qatar resume. Attacks spreading to the Gulf of Oman widen the danger zone.

Nobel Prize
Fact

Economics to Robert E. Hall and Michael Woodford 'for microeconomic foundations of macroeconomic stabilisation'

What happened

The Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences awarded the 2026 Sveriges Riksbank Prize in Economic Sciences in Memory of Alfred Nobel on Monday 12 October to Robert E. Hall, born 1943, of Stanford University, and Michael Woodford, born 1955, of Columbia University, in equal shares, 'for microeconomic foundations of macroeconomic stabilisation' (nobelprize.org press release). The academy says they rebuilt macroeconomic models on how households and businesses actually behave, rather than on statistical links between inflation, output and spending, so that well-timed fiscal and monetary policy can smooth booms and busts. Woodford is best known for 'Interest and Prices', the textbook basis of New Keynesian monetary models used by central banks. Hall is known for work on consumption, labour markets and business-cycle dating. The prize is 12 million Swedish kronor.

Context (what it sits on)

The award lands while central banks, including the US Federal Reserve, are raising rates again to fight inflation driven by an oil shock. It closes Nobel week: Medicine to Deisseroth, Hegemann and Nagel, Physics to Francis Halzen, Chemistry to Henri Kagan and Kenso Soai, Literature to Anne Carson, Peace to Navi Pillay.

Forward (months–years)

Prize ceremonies in Stockholm and Oslo on 10 December.
